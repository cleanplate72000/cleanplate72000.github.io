Mini Metro Review: Subway Genius (2026)

Quick verdict — 4.5 / 5. Mini Metro is the rare mobile game that earns its calm reputation: you draw coloured subway lines between stations, watch a city grow around them, and quietly panic when a station overcrowds. It costs $0.99, has no ads and no in-app purchases, and is rated 4.6 stars from more than 74,000 players. It loses half a star for a thin tutorial and controls that can feel fiddly on a busy map — but for a thoughtful, beautiful strategy game, it is one of the best buys on Google Play.

Mini Metro, from New Zealand developer Dinosaur Polo Club, asks a simple question: can you keep a growing city moving? You start with a handful of stations — circles, triangles, squares — and a few lines to connect them. Trains run back and forth, carrying passengers to the station shape they want. Then new stations appear, the city spreads across rivers, and your neat little network starts to strain. Your job is to redraw, reroute and rethink before a station fills up and the game ends.

The numbers explain why it is still trending a decade after launch. The live Google Play listing shows about 4.6 stars from over 74,000 ratings, and independent tracker AppBrain puts it at 3.7 million installs, with roughly 32,000 installs in the last 30 days and a #2 ranking in Top Paid Simulation in the United States. It has been on Play since October 2016, the current version is 2.57.1, updated 11 June 2026, and it needs Android 5.0 or higher. The download is about 261 MB, which is large for such a minimalist-looking game, so grab it on Wi-Fi if your data is tight.

The whole game in one picture: coloured lines, shaped stations, and a city that will not stop growing.

How it actually plays

There is no building, no money to manage and no timetable to set. You drag a line between two stations, and a train starts running. Passengers appear as small shapes: a triangle passenger wants a triangle station, and so on. When a station gets too crowded for too long, it is game over. That is the entire rulebook — and it is enough, because the city never sits still. Random growth means no two games play out the same way, and every new station forces a decision: extend a line, start a new one, or spend one of your limited resources on an extra train, carriage, tunnel or line.

Early maps look peaceful. Give it a few in-game weeks and every line is doing three jobs at once.

Modes, cities and upgrades

Normal mode is the scored, week-by-week challenge most players start with. Endless mode removes the game-over pressure for a relaxed session, while Extreme mode is the unforgiving version for players who want the hardest test. Creative mode, added more recently, lets you build a metro exactly how you want it, and a Daily Challenge pits you against the world on the same map every day. There are over two dozen real-world cities to play, each with its own geography — rivers, islands and layouts that change how a sensible network looks.

Upgrades arrive weekly in a run and are always a trade-off: an extra line, an extra train, a carriage, or a tunnel or bridge to cross water. Spend them well and a struggling network breathes again; spend them badly and you will feel it two weeks later. The game also includes colourblind and night modes, and a responsive soundtrack by Disasterpeace that is generated by your metro system itself — trains and stations become part of the music, which is a lovely touch that suits the game's calm surface.

Real cities, real geography: rivers and coastlines decide where your lines can sensibly go.

Pros and cons

What we liked: a genuinely original idea, executed with restraint; no ads and no in-app purchases, ever; a beautiful minimalist look that stays readable even on a phone screen; real depth — planning, prioritising and adapting under pressure; and modes for both relaxing play and serious challenge.

What to watch out for: the tutorial is basic, and several reviewers say it leaves much of the game unexplained — expect to learn by failing a few runs. Dragging lines on a crowded map can be fiddly, and it is easy to connect the wrong station when lines overlap. The difficulty ramps up sharply, so first-time players should not expect an easy win. And note the developer's own warning: Mini Metro is incompatible with some Bluetooth headphones, so if you hear no audio over Bluetooth, disconnect them and restart the game.

When it works, it really works — a dense, humming network you built one drag at a time.

Pricing

Mini Metro costs $0.99 on Google Play, and that is the whole price: the developer states clearly that there are no ads and no in-app purchases. For a game with this much replay value — dozens of cities, daily challenges and endless mode — that is excellent value, and it is one of the main reasons players keep recommending it years after release. There is also an App Store version, rated 4.9 stars, if you play on iPhone or iPad as well.

Download

Download Mini Metro on Google Play

If you like games that reward planning over reflexes, Mini Metro is an easy recommendation: a BAFTA-nominated, IGF award-winning subway simulator that is calm to look at, tense to play, and fairly priced with nothing else to buy.
