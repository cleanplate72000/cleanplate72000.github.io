Monument Valley Review: Pure Illusion (2026)

Quick verdict — 4.8 / 5. Monument Valley is still the benchmark for a beautiful mobile puzzle game: you guide the silent princess Ida through hand-crafted monuments built from impossible geometry, twisting and dragging the world itself until a path appears. It costs $3.99, has no ads, and is rated about 4.9 stars from more than 240,000 players. It loses a little for its short main game and its large download, and for charging extra for the Forgotten Shores expansion — but as a calm, clever, one-time purchase, it remains one of the best buys on Google Play.

Monument Valley, from ustwo games, starts with a simple, almost wordless idea. Ida stands on a monument that cannot exist in the real world: staircases climb into thin air, bridges cross gaps that should not be crossable, and a tower that looks solid from one angle turns out to be an optical illusion. Your job is to rotate, slide and unfold parts of the building until the impossible becomes a walkable path. There is no timer, no lives system and no fail state to rush you. The pleasure is in the moment a route clicks into place.

The numbers show why it is still trending more than a decade after its first release on Google Play in May 2014. The live Play listing shows about 4.9 stars, AppBrain puts total installs at about 5.6 million (Play itself lists the 5M+ band), with roughly 13,000 installs in the last 30 days, and AppBrain ranked it #2 in Top Paid Puzzle in the United Kingdom on 26 October 2026. The current version is 3.8.112, updated 12 October 2026, and the download is about 290 MB — large for a puzzle game, so download it on Wi-Fi. It is rated Everyone on Play, which makes it a genuinely family-safe pick.

The first lesson of Monument Valley: if a staircase seems to lead nowhere, try turning the world instead.

How it actually plays

Every level is a small, self-contained monument. Ida walks automatically along any path you open up for her; you tap where she should go, and you manipulate the architecture to make that route possible. A platform that looks disconnected may connect when you rotate a tower. A staircase that ends in mid-air may line up with a doorway once a section of the building slides into place. The enigmatic Crow People wander some levels too, blocking paths or patrolling walkways, so you sometimes have to wait, move them out of the way, or find a route around them.

That sounds simple, and the controls are — twist and drag, as the developer puts it, designed to be easy for everyone to pick up. The thinking is what takes time. Monument Valley never explains its puzzles with long tutorials. Instead, each monument teaches you a new trick by letting you try it: a rotating pillar here, a sliding block there, a bridge that only exists when two impossible shapes align. Because there is no clock, you can stare at a level for as long as you like, turn it over in your mind, and enjoy the quiet satisfaction of solving it yourself.

Crow People are not enemies to fight — they are moving obstacles to work around, which keeps the game calm rather than stressful.

What makes it different

Most puzzle games give you a grid, a timer or a score to chase. Monument Valley gives you architecture. Its look is inspired, in the developer's own words, by minimalist 3D design, optical illusions and palaces and temples from around the world, and every monument is a unique, hand-crafted world to explore. Colours shift from level to level — soft teal one moment, deep purple the next, warm sand the next — so each chapter feels like walking into a different painting.

Sound matters here as much as the visuals. The Play description notes that audio reacts to your manipulation of the world, creating a surreal soundscape, and it recommends headphones. That is good advice: with sound on, turning a tower or sliding a platform produces a gentle, musical response that makes the world feel alive, even though the game itself stays quiet and unhurried. There is also cloud save, so you can synchronise progress across devices, and the game is optimised for both phones and tablets — on a larger screen, the small paths and moving pieces are easier to follow.

Later monuments add moving machinery: rotate the central mechanism and the whole level rearranges itself.

Story, chapters and expansions

Monument Valley tells its story without much dialogue. Ida is a silent princess travelling through mysterious monuments, uncovering hidden paths and, in the developer's phrase, learning about forgiveness. The mood is dreamlike rather than dramatic: you are meant to feel curious, not hurried. That is also why the game works so well as a short, relaxing session — you can play one monument, put the phone down, and come back later without losing track of anything.

Two additions are worth knowing about before you buy. The Play listing says Ida's Dream is now available, and Forgotten Shores adds eight new chapters of adventure and illusion as a separate purchase. That means the $3.99 base price does not buy absolutely everything in one go. For most players that is a fair trade — the base game is a complete, satisfying experience on its own — but if you finish it and want more, budget for the expansion rather than expecting it to be included.

Some of the best levels are the darkest and simplest: one impossible shape, one quiet puzzle, no clutter at all.

Pros and cons

What we liked: a genuinely original idea that still feels fresh; beautiful, hand-crafted monuments that look like works of art; calm, untimed puzzles that respect the player's intelligence; no ads at all; a soundscape that rewards headphones; cloud save and tablet support; and a price that buys a complete game, not a stream of consumables.

What to watch out for: the main game is short compared with endless puzzle games — players who want hundreds of levels will finish it and want more. The download is about 290 MB, which is large for a puzzle title. The Forgotten Shores expansion costs extra, so the full experience is more than $3.99. And the gentle pace is the point, so if you prefer fast, competitive or action-heavy games, this quiet, thoughtful style may not be your speed.

Pricing

Monument Valley costs $3.99 on Google Play, with no ads. It does list in-app purchases, because the Forgotten Shores expansion is sold separately, as noted above. There is no subscription and no energy system: once you own the base game, you can play it at your own pace, offline, for as long as you like. For a game rated 4.9 stars by hundreds of thousands of players, and still ranking among the top paid puzzle games in 2026, that is strong value — especially compared with free games that interrupt every few minutes with adverts.
