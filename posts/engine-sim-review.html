Engine Sim Review: Rev 28 Engines Live (2026)

Quick verdict — 4.5 / 5. Engine Sim - Sound Simulator is a sound-first driving toy done with unusual care: 28 engines are synthesised live from how hard you press, not looped recordings, and you drive them yourself with a clutch, an H-pattern shifter and tuning that visibly changes performance. It earns 4.5, not 5, because it is a simulator of sound and feel rather than a full driving game, and because full-screen ads between engine switches are part of the free deal unless you pay once to remove them — but for car-sound fans, it is the most convincing free version of this idea on Play right now.

Engine Sim - Sound Simulator, from developer Takasawa Dynamics, starts with a simple promise on its Google Play page: put a finger on the screen and a 660cc turbo kei car wakes up, then slide over to a 4-rotor, a W16 or a racing V10 that screams to 19,000 rpm. There are 28 engines in all, and the reason the app is climbing fast is that the sound is not a recording on a loop. Every sound is made live, so the same rev never sounds exactly the same twice, and the throttle follows your finger pressure: rest it for a soft idle, floor it for a full scream.

The numbers behind that climb are striking for an app that only arrived on Google Play in September 2026. AppBrain's 25 October 2026 snapshot puts it at 260,000 installs, all of them in the last 30 days — roughly 11,000 a day — while the Play listing itself shows a rating of 4.77 stars from 3,671 ratings. It is also ranked in the top free Simulation charts in several countries, including #15 in Saudi Arabia. That is a fast, genuinely loved start, not a slow burn — and it is why it earns a review this week.

How it plays

You start with the inline-4, and every core feature is available from the start, with no ads on the driving screen itself. The dashboard is the game: a colour RPM gauge with a rev-limiter cut, a gear display, and brake and throttle pedals that respond to pressure. Haptics follow revs, load and shifts, so the phone itself tells you when a shift lands well or when you have got it wrong.

The driving controls are where Engine Sim separates itself from a simple soundboard. You can use an H-pattern shifter or paddles, switching any time, and the clutch pedal has a real bite point: let it out too fast and the engine stalls. Practice rev-matching — brake, blip the throttle, shift — and a clean shift is silent, while a botched one thumps through the driveline. That is a real skill to learn, and it is the reason the app keeps people coming back after the novelty of the first rev wears off.

28 engines, from kei car to W16

The engine list is the heart of the app, and it is impressively wide. The Play listing names single-cylinder engines, a V-twin, two 660cc kei turbos, inline-4, inline-5 and inline-6 engines, a diesel truck inline-6, a boxer-4, a flat-6, V6, three V8s, V10, V12 and W16 engines, plus rotaries from 2-rotor to 4-rotor and a racing V10 that revs to 19,000 rpm. The screenshots show the same range in the garage list, from a 1.6-litre inline-4 economy engine to a 5.0-litre V8 and a 6.2-litre big-torque V8.

Each engine is built from per-cylinder firing, uneven exhaust timing and pipe resonance, which is why a V8 does not just sound like a louder inline-4. A straight-pipe mode adds pops and bangs on lift-off, and a back turbine gives compressor surge. The listing's own tip is worth following: headphones bring out the fine detail that a phone speaker flattens.

Key features

  • 28 live-synthesised engines — sound generated in real time, never a looped recording
  • Pressure-sensitive throttle — soft idle to full scream, following your finger
  • Real driving controls — H-pattern shifter or paddles, clutch with a bite point, rev-matching and stalling
  • Tuning that matters — mufflers, intakes, headers, superchargers, cams and ECU maps change 0–100 km/h times, top speed and the torque curve
  • Chassis tuning — weight, aero and flywheel shared across engines, plus race clutch, race flywheel, carbon body and race aero
  • Progression without a subscription — miles from driving unlock engines and parts, with daily tasks, weekly challenges, a trophy exchange and a Pit Stop summary after each drive
  • Share a run — export a drive as a video with the engine sound, or as audio only

Tuning and progression

Tuning is not cosmetic here. Swap a muffler, change an intake or remap the ECU and the app shows the effect on 0–100 km/h time, top speed and the torque curve straight away — the tuning screenshot shows a car at 3.6 seconds to 100 km/h with a racing muffler fitted and straight-pipe mode switched on. That immediate feedback turns tuning from guesswork into experimentation: try a part, rev it, watch the numbers move.

Progression works the same honest way. Miles from driving unlock engines and parts through an unlock tree, and garage miles keep building while the app is closed. After each drive, a Pit Stop summary shows distance, miles earned and personal bests, and daily tasks, a login bonus and weekly challenges give short-term goals. The next unowned V6, V8 or V12 can be rented for 10 minutes after a short video ad, and straight-pipe mode can be tried for 7 minutes the same way — a fair try-before-you-buy system for an app with no subscriptions at all.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Live-synthesised sound that genuinely changes with pressure, revs and tuning — not a soundboard
  • Real driving skill to learn: clutch bite point, rev-matching, stalling and clean shifts
  • 28 engines with real variety, from a 660cc kei turbo to a 19,000 rpm racing V10
  • Strong early verdict from players: 4.77 stars from over 3,600 ratings
  • No subscriptions — progression by driving, plus a one-time purchase option
  • Small download at around 18 MB, and free to start with every core feature

Cons

  • It is a sound and feel simulator, not a driving game — there is no road, traffic or racing to look at
  • Full-screen ads appear between engine switches unless you make the one-time purchase to remove them
  • Some engines and parts sit behind rentals, video ads or a purchase, so the full garage is not instant
  • A Parts Crate with shown odds is part of progression — check it before spending miles or money
  • Very new, so long-term depth and update support are still unproven, and one reviewer wished for account sync across devices

The honest caveats

First, set expectations correctly: Engine Sim is about hearing and feeling engines, not about driving somewhere. If you want scenery, opponents or a career mode, this is not that game, and judging it as one would miss the point. Second, the free deal has a visible price: the driving screen itself is ad-free, but full-screen ads show between engine switches until you pay once to remove them, and rentals run on short video ads. That is fair and clearly stated on the Play page, but it is worth knowing before you install.

Third, the app is only about six weeks old, so its impressive rating comes from a still-growing base, and its long-term support is unproven. AppBrain notes the app requests permissions for ad identifiers, network state, foreground service, notifications and billing, so check the privacy policy if ad tracking matters to you. None of that outweighs what the app does well — but it is the honest context behind a very high early score.

Pricing

Engine Sim - Sound Simulator is free to download on Google Play, with no subscriptions. The Play listing checked for this review states that engines and parts can also be bought with an optional one-time in-app purchase, and that a one-time purchase removes the full-screen ads shown between engine switches, while rentals and mile rewards stay available either way. No purchase price was shown on the listing checked, so check the in-app price before buying anything. It carries an Everyone content rating, and AppBrain's 25 October 2026 snapshot lists the download at around 18 MB — small enough to try without thinking twice.

Download

Download Engine Sim - Sound Simulator on Google Play

If you love the sound of a V12 or a rotary but have never sat behind one, Engine Sim is the cheapest, most convincing way to hear what they actually feel like to drive — and the clutch, the rev-matching and the tuning give it far more depth than a soundboard.
